# The Cold Start Problem

## Origin
Andrew Chen, *The Cold Start Problem* (book, 2021). Codifies 15 years of network-effects thinking into a single framework.

## The five stages of network-effect businesses

1. **The Cold Start Problem** — the chicken-and-egg phase. You need users to attract users; you have neither.
2. **The Tipping Point** — when the network reaches enough density to sustain itself on its own pull.
3. **Escape Velocity** — sustained growth driven by network effects, not paid acquisition.
4. **Hitting the Ceiling** — saturation problems: growth slows, retention erodes, the magic stops compounding.
5. **The Moat** — long-term defensibility; how mature networks defend against new entrants.

## The Atomic Network concept
The smallest possible network that, on its own, is engaging enough to retain users.
- For Uber: drivers and riders in a single city, dense enough that pickup time is acceptable.
- For Slack: a single team where most communication happens in Slack.
- For Tinder: a single college campus where there are enough matches to be addictive.
- **Build one atomic network at a time.** Don't try to launch globally.

## Why a16z uses it
- The canonical lens for evaluating consumer + marketplace + social companies.
- Filters out "viral hopium" companies (those without a real atomic network thesis).
- Sets expectations: early-stage network-effect businesses look weak by traditional metrics. Look for atomic-network density, not aggregate users.

## How to apply it as an evaluator
- Ask: "What's the atomic network for this company?"
- "Have they tipped one atomic network? If yes, can they replicate it?"
- "Is growth coming from genuine network effect, or from paid acquisition that will collapse?"
- "If they hit the ceiling, what's plan B for unlocking the next growth phase?"

## Anti-patterns
- "We'll go global from day one" without a single tipped atomic network.
- "Our network effect is data" — data is sometimes a moat, but often weaker than founders claim.
- Confusing scale of users with network effect — most large user bases are NOT network effects.
